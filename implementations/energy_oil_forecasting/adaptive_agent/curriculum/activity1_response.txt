Based on a rigorous rolling backtest spanning the entire year of 2025 (251 daily forecast origins), here is a complete analysis of the performance of a simple trend-projection forecaster under various volatility regimes.

---

### **1. Methodology and Data Preparation**

*   **Data Fetching:** Daily WTI close prices (ticker: `CL=F`) were retrieved via `yfinance` for 2025. To ensure that 21-day rolling calculations were fully populated from the very first business day of 2025, price data was fetched starting from **November 1, 2024**.
*   **Volatility and Regimes:** 21-day rolling realized volatility (annualized %) was computed as:
    $$\sigma_{21} = \text{std}(\text{daily log returns}) \times \sqrt{252} \times 100$$
    Days were classified into regimes using the user-defined thresholds:
    *   **Low:** $<15\%$ annualized vol
    *   **Medium:** $15\% - 30\%$ annualized vol
    *   **Elevated:** $30\% - 50\%$ annualized vol
    *   **Extreme:** $>50\%$ annualized vol
*   **Simulation Parameters:** For each business day $t$ in 2025, we simulated a forecaster projecting prices to horizons of **5, 10, and 21 business days** using:
    1.  **Linear Trend Projection ($W=30$):** Standard 30-day lookback window.
    2.  **Linear Trend Projection ($W=15$):** Shortened 15-day lookback window (adaptive for high volatility).
    3.  **Flat-Trend (Random Walk):** Forecast is simply the current day's price ($F_{t+h} = P_t$).
    4.  **Theoretical Random-Walk Standard Error:** Computed as $\sigma_{\text{daily, USD}} \times \sqrt{h}$ for comparison.

### **4. Structural Findings and Key Insights**

#### **A. Largest Errors Occur at Long Horizons in Extreme Volatility**
Under standard trend projection ($W=30$), the largest errors are concentrated at the **21-day horizon during the extreme volatility regime** (MAE of **\$9.12/bbl**, RMSE of **\$9.41/bbl**). At long horizons, linear extrapolation is highly sensitive to the slope fitted in-sample, causing point forecasts to overshoot drastically when prices turn around.

#### **B. Severe and Regime-Dependent Directional Bias**
Linear trend extrapolation suffers from severe **lagging bias** that switches sign depending on the regime's price action:
*   **Extreme Vol Regime (Overprediction Bias / -$5.75 Mean Error at 21 bd):** Extreme volatility in 2025 occurred during temporary price spikes (e.g., late June, where prices peaked near \$73 before returning to \$64). The trend model fit a steep upward slope and projected it forward. When the spike mean-reverted, the model severely overpredicted the price (Actual $\ll$ Forecast), leading to a highly negative Mean Error.
*   **Elevated Vol Regime (Underprediction Bias / +$2.85 Mean Error at 21 bd):** Elevated volatility periods often occurred during or directly after sharp selloffs (e.g., in April, where WTI fell from \$71 to \$58). The trend model fit a steep downward slope and projected a continued collapse. When WTI prices stabilized and began to base or rebound, the model underpredicted the price (Actual $\gg$ Forecast), leading to a highly positive Mean Error.

#### **C. Shortening the Trend Window (W=15) Worsens Performance**
While shortening the trend window is a common heuristic in high volatility, doing so actually **increased forecast errors** across almost all horizons in 2025. For example, at the 21-day horizon in the elevated regime, the MAE rose from **\$7.33 ($W=30$)** to **\$10.25 ($W=15$)**. In high-volatility regimes, a shorter lookback is incredibly noisy and "chases" transient daily fluctuations, leading to highly volatile and erratic projections.

#### **D. Flat-Trend (Random Walk) Consistently Outperforms Trend Projection**
Astonishingly, **a simple flat-trend model (forecasting $P_{t+h} = P_t$) outperformed trend projection in every single regime and horizon**.
*   This outperformance is most dramatic at the 21-day horizon in the **extreme regime**, where flat-trend achieved an MAE of **\$2.52/bbl** (a **72% reduction** in error compared to trend-projection's \$9.12/bbl) with virtually zero bias (Mean Error of **+\$0.02**).
*   Furthermore, the flat-trend's empirical error standard deviation was significantly *lower* than the theoretical random-walk standard error ($\sigma_{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{h}$). For example, at 21 bd in the extreme regime, the theoretical standard error is **\$6.82**, but the empirical flat-trend standard deviation was only **\$3.14**. This strongly indicates that WTI price fluctuations are heavily **mean-reverting** over multi-week horizons.
